  Cody 

My keys slide out of the ignition, and after a long, sleepless night, I realize I'm sitting in Navidson-Monroe's dirt lot, seventeen minutes early to clocking in.

But I don't think I'll be clocking in today, because maybe they shouldn't know I'm here. I've been consistent for the last couple months: maybe I can just pass it off as a convenient accident for this day they're set to let me go, as I bide my time at my workstation, waiting for the perfect moment to put a suit on and rush to that distant conference room in eight hours.

I find a tremor in my hands when I reach beneath the seat to slide it back at a distance: about as much as an airline would give if you threw an extra hundred dollars at your ticket. The passenger-side window is curtained by the suit I last wore to present my senior project: kept pristine in a plastic bag from the cleaners, with a blue, paper tag stapled inside one of the button holes dated almost three years ago.

The space between the pedals and the seat is still poor enough to keep my legs from trembling as pitch the seat back, looking up to remember I have a sun roof for the first time in ages. Maybe I'd find sleep if I wasn't energized by a five-hour energy I guzzled to keep my eyes open: Katiya and I didn't let each other sleep, whether it was the pent-up passion on the long passage from haters to lovers, or the discomfort in the thought of sleep bringing us to the next day faster.

The thoughts about every way this mission can go wrong fill my mind for another fifteen minutes, until I can't take it any longer, and I step out in my father's clothes, removing the Pelican case from my trunk to store it under the car until Kei or Reed can inch it further to the aerospace tower on break.

But I need to get myself there, and yet all I ever want is to stay there in the safety of my car: finding that the feeling rhymes with that incessant drive to langush at the lowest state of energy; the feeling I find on nights where the emptiness is at its worst.

But the thing is that I told people I was doing this. I'll lose my home if I don't try — and for the first time in ages, it feels like I'm being pulled more by the luminous threads of a future dream than threatened by a scalding brand of failure.

And I think I actually want a result from something for the first time in forever, and I really want it, so much that I withdraw the old suit from the passenger side of the car and take my technician's bag in my other hand, barely able to contain a box of my brother's uniformed dress shoes that might still fit me.

I approach the door with a little piece of everyone upon me, slowing my roll then speeding up, then slow down again, as I normalize my speed with the stream of all the remaining technicians holding the door open for each other.

"You tryna take my fuckin' job, Dunsmore?" One of the technicians jokes, punching my other shoulder with his calloused hand. "Tryna get to floor manager so you can re-hire your dad?"

They chide each other in terms of crass deprecation, and it's why I've actually come to like my time on the floor.

"No, it's so I can fire ugly pukes like you," I say, earning a loud laugh from the other grisly technicians, distracting some suit-wearers from the Human Resources division.

"Your pops told me about your thing today," the same guy says. "You need someone to cover your work or stir a ruckus, let me know!"

"Or me!" Another says.

They clap me on the back as I sneak to my work station, hanging the suit from Dad's toolbench with a picture of my brother and I: the same wallpaper on my brother's old iPod that I lost. My father's advice echoes in my head, to keep those hands moving or else they'll come for you.

So I grab a wrench, cranking away at the drawings I once helped create with something other than despair, as I toil in the shadow of the reaper.

  Katiya 

The chill of an open window isn't the first sensation I feel when I wake. Maybe I stirred a little between frames of a dream when he rose to go to work — cracking a window since my small bedroom was warm without Cody's creature comforts of conditioned air — all I really remember is the pronouncement of how he loves me, no matter how this story ends.

The first sensation is sound: hearing the raucous crash of something heavy before I planned to wake. The knocker stopped rapping my door after the incident in Hillfar. And usually, Titus and Sylvia are so silent that I hardly hear them before the morning begins.

I think it's another break-in before I hear the voices announce themselves in the early hours. I could only presume they'd be smitten by the immeasurable power of the refugees, but when I don't hear an answer, I know exactly what's going on.

An inclement blizzard glows with enough of the morning light for me to look down and notice I'm not wearing anything. The commotion grows downstairs as my eyes refuse to leave the round door handle: familiar voices that aren't my allies, but familiar from somewhere else, and as I throw the nearest pair of civilian clothes on, the only thing I can think of is the meeting I promised Jade and Aliaki before danger befalls Gaffesend.

I wear enough to run, without even the shoes downstairs to keep my feet warm, deciding I'll rely instead on the Essence to melt the snow beneath my feet. I curse and I struggle with words that don't even belong to my world, throwing the window open further as flakes filter in, searching the room for the small sphere that vibrates with otherworldly energy.

Fuck, where is it?

I check beneath Cody's clothes. I check the corner, I check the bathroom and the vanity, and just when I turn to find it next to Cody's little music box, the door handle turns gingerly.

It's then that I make the choice on Rayna's gambit before I can even think. I eye the window still fluttering with drapes, trying to remember the landscape below and where the latticed garden fence terminates in sharp spears, but I don't have the time.

My heart pulses, and I decide to move instead of hesitate, just as the sound of my footfalls brings the intruders to bash the door open in splinters. The cloak that once hid my shame in the beginning flutters behind me as my bare feet take three long strides toward the windowsill, trying to calculate how I'll shape my body to fit through the small window as I haven't even the mental space to think of how I'll land.

"Katiya, stop!" My brother yells, and then the rage of betrayal makes me not even care how I'll resolve the next few seconds. I throw myself through the window as his fingers tangle between the fabric of the cloak. It's only when half my body is beyond the threshold of the window that I see Titus and Sylvia being marshalled by the Royal Guard, and I catch a sudden source of slack in the cape I thought might keep me warm.

"Gods damn it, Katiya," Erik says, as the sudden slack makes me flip forwards and slam against the wooden siding.

I see a constellation of stars like Cody's little lights beneath the fabric when my head hits the wall. I choke in the pocket of recycled air, thrashing as I struggle for enough breath to curse him.

And I find it.

"You're killing everyone, Erik!" I manage. "You have to let me—" and I begin to choke on the fabric as he tries to pull back in the window. I free my left arm, grabbing the canvas noose to try to free my throat before I draw the breath to remove my other arm.

"You're going to get yourself killed, Katiya," he says. "Bertram wasn't your fault!"

"This isn't about Bertram," I manage on a rasping exhale. "They're coming." I tuck my arm further through the sleeve, and eventually my chin is the only thing hanging as I near the windowsill: playing dead-fish tug-of-war with a few other guards that try to help Erik even the odds.

And then I slip through the fabric wearing summer clothes. The Essence scalds my circuits enough to evaporate the snowflakes as I collapse into the snowdrift, springing to my feet to try to run as I feel the sphere still swirling in my pocket.

And then I realize why Titus and Sylvia never tried to protest.

Twenty guards stand watch over the small terrace, forming a wall with their plated armor. Two of them grab my arms to bind my wrists as Erik pulls up the last of the cloak, peeking out the window.

"You're not a deserter here. You're being held until you can stand tribunal," he cries in relief. "This is for your own protection."

  Cody 

The monkey suit strangles me when I wrangle it on in a narrow stall of the technician's washroom. The stench of shit and cigarettes makes it hard to clock if I've applied too much cologne — and after the mirror got punched out by a man called Brucifer, I have to balance my phone on the seat cover holder to make sure my tie is straight.

But the tight collar offers a feeling of protection, dressing even nicer than I did as an engineer. The coolant-stained work shirt sits in a pungent pouch of the briefcase I'm carrying, along with the cologne sampler and some deodorant as I try to plan my egress, thirty minutes early to the end of my shift.

My neck strains from spending seven-and-a-half hours looking over my shoulder, more sensitive to axe-talk than a pine tree in the middle of a ski run. But I made it this far: by now, Kei should have left the pelican case at the top of the tower, and all that's left is to ascend.

I leave the washroom wearing an outfit I've only worn once before. The shop is warm despite the winter's cold, and whether it's from the nerves or long sleeves and pants, I can already feel myself sweat.

It's only times like these that you realize how many unfamiliar faces walk these halls. Every white-collared stranger is a threat, like those flesh walkers in Katiya's world — and I'm waiting for that tell of recognition as I try to slip between them, racking my mind for a memory of the office layout.

It's a quick pass through the engineering wing, through the cubicle farm, then out the door to the Aero building's glass elevator instead of racing my way through the lower floors. Then thirty minutes until it begins, and in that time I figure it out from there, since the engineers never decided on a conference room.

I climb a familiar set of grated metal stairs to rise to the battery division's mezzanine. Peering in the window, I find Reed's desk empty, even though he promised me that he'd be there.

I scan my keycard. The light flashes red. I scan it again, and as I try it a third time, I feel my smart watch vibrate on my wrist.

It's not a text.

It's a call from Reed.

I curse as I fumble through my pockets to find my gym earbuds to pop in an ear. Cornered in a high position, I rush back down the stairs as my mind scrambles over an alternate route, waiting for the call to connect when this building is a fucking Faraday cage.

It stops ringing.

"Reed?" I ask.

No answer. But I can hear a rustling.

My grasp tightens on the briefcase. I can feel the eyes of everyone in the midday shuffle as I decide I'll go for the rear warehouse door instead of going through Engineering.

I hear distant voices over the call. "He tried to use his badge at the engineering door. He didn't leave! He's still in the building."

My heart pulses. The technicians jeer at me when they see a suit with such high blood pressure.

"Get Security," a voice demands. "We have our witness. Now he needs to sign the form."

The badge is useless to me. I decide to dart down a dead-end hallway and scan the badge half-a-dozen more times before pitching it in the trash and rapidly retracing my steps, turning the corner in my disguise as two HR goons take the bait.

Government contracts means a hefty budget for guards, and I realize I'm far from safety as three more uniforms look down at their phones while scanning the crowd, ten yards ahead of me, right in front of the only cardless door.

I feel a meaty hand on my collar, and it startles me until I realize it's too calloused to be an office worker.

He pulls me through the blue-plastic weld shop curtain. His knuckles are bloody: surely from punching the mirror in the break room, but he's my dad's best friend.

"Are you stuck, kid?" Bruce asks me. "Your dad told me about your stupid plan."

"I can't get to the Aero wing," I say, but he coaxes me towards a large metal weldment: one of the battery enclosures I helped design.

"This has to go to second-story inspection for tomorrow," he says, and he opens the door of the cabinet: its guts still unfilled with wires.

"You don't mean—"

"Kid, I stopped giving two fucks about OSHA when those office pukes stopped buying us gloves," he says.

I step inside, expecting the hydraulic forklift to hoist the battery cabinet like an elevator, but the whir of something else makes my stomach turn harder.

I fall to the floor of the cabinet as the bridge crane hoists the metal box two stores into the air. Through the vents, I see the three guards refuse to leave the door: my employee profile picture on their screens as Brucifer hoists the cabinet to the engineering wing's inspection balcony.

I see stars when my head slams against the steel: not enough to concuss, but enough to stun, as the ringing in my ears fades out to the fifteen-minute mark of Reed's call.

"The floor," they say. "He's on the floor. The rear door is blocked: either you catch him, or the gate guard stops him."

Brucifer sets the enclosure gently on the painted target. I peek through the door like a nerd hiding in a high school locker, and when the huge weldment settles, I race for the only badge-free door in the engineering wing.

The door opens.

I rub the back of my head as I speed-walk through a familiar set of cubicles. The exit is right past the Aspen conference room, and though I hear a commotion, I decide to make a break for it, accelerating my footfalls as the sound over my headphones begins to mesh with the voices in the room.

It's too late to respond when the director of Human Resources exits the room, finding her mark standing right in front of her.

"Looking sharp today, Mr. Dunsmore," she says. "Where are you headed?"

"And where would you go?" Aryn asks. "That was just so stupid, Katiya. You're no better than my sister. You're the same type of coward!"

My knuckles are white when I grasp the bars. "A coward? Aryn, listen to me: in a couple hours, this cell will be the safest room in all of Gaffesend, and yet, for the first time in my life, I feel more afraid of what's on the other side."

Titus and Sylvia sit across from me in separate cells, behind Aryn, listening to her taunt me as a way to blow off steam. "I was going to take a scouting quest. But I thought you should get an earful from me instead," she says.

Titus interjects, "if there's any time for an act of civil disobedience, it's now."

"Just say the word," Sylvia says, and maybe Aryn's so hot with disappointment that she can't feel how the sudden rise in Essence feathers our circuits. Sylvia's hands clutch the lattice of my stepfather's ironwork — her crimson eyes burning with something ancient — and I watch her fingers begin to slide through the iron like butter. Titus pulls from something similar.

But I interrupt them.

"Not now," I say. "It will be a losing fight if it's only us."

Aryn scoffs. "Even if you broke out, how would you get past me? You couldn't even beat me in a match!" I look for a sense of sadistic pleasure in her face, but only find an overwhelming sense of hurt in her soul. "You're my closest friend, and I'm not letting you go this time. Not to cower like Rayna."

"And what is that cowardice to you?" I ask. "Because I'm terrified right now." I show my trembling hands. "See? I'm terrified because I already decided months ago what I'm going to do today, and that wasn't running away."

"Your duty is to stand trial. And I'll provide everything I can to clear you — Jullian and ai both, and your brother, and even if it doesn't work," Aryn's voice strains, "then...then I know it's going to be alright."

"So you're afraid," I say.

"No, of course not."

"Complicity isn't courage, Aryn. I was complicit in doing the minimum by giving up on scouting. And now, even if I could go back and relive the day Bertram left, I wouldn't have done it any different."

"Then I hope you don't say that at trial," she says. "This new you could have put you at the top of your class! We could graduate, then save lives on the other side."

"There won't be any lives left to save," I say. "That bill is coming due early, because chaos doesn't wait for the spaces between order."

She pauses. Something in her can't resist the question. "And what do you think is coming?"

"A small battalion of Ahkvas that's grown by the week. About four hundred, attacking from the west, when they know we're all watching the frozen lake." I heard the whispers over the sphere on my pocket they thought was just a trinket.

"I know. Rayna told me," she says, flicking the end of her tail without rhythm.

I grab at the bars again. pressing my face closer to hers. "If you keep me here and I'm right, you'll have to live with choosing procedure over people."

"But . . . I can't just let you go," she says.

Titus says, "of course you can!"

"You don't have the momentum, no matter how powerful your friends are here," Aryn says. "Four hundred? And that is, assuming I believe it."

"All I want is an audience with Aldrin, and you can take me there," I say. "Three of us is a coerced prison break. But just you and me looks like procedure."

She still hesitates. Her sharp nails bite her flesh, so I give her one last push.

"Rayna didn't fail because she ran," I tell her. "She failed because no one helped her when it mattered the most."

"Then you better not be wrong," she says. She looks left and right, then twists the key in the lock, blocking the open door with her body as if she's trying to keep a pet from escaping.

"You're making the right decision," I say, as I hear something click behind her.

She swats at my left wrist with a hook made of metal, and I only realize it's one end of a handcuff when she raises her right arm in front of me.

"But I don't think you won't try something," she says. "You have a lot to prove."
